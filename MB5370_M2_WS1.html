Mean Body Mass and Variation Using Standard Deviation

Mean body mass was compared across penguin species and islands. Points represent group means, with error bars showing one standard deviation around each mean.

# Summarise body mass and pipe directly into a plot
mass_compare_plot <- penguins |>
  group_by(species, island) |>
  summarise(
    mean_mass = mean(body_mass_g, na.rm = TRUE),
    sd_mass = sd(body_mass_g, na.rm = TRUE),
    n = n(),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  ggplot(aes(x = species, y = mean_mass, colour = island)) +
  geom_point(size = 3) +
  geom_errorbar(
    aes(
      ymin = mean_mass - sd_mass,
      ymax = mean_mass + sd_mass
    ),
    width = 0.2
  ) +
  labs(
    title = "Mean Body Mass Across Palmer Penguin Species and Islands",
    subtitle = "Error bars represent ±1 standard deviation",
    x = "Species",
    y = "Mean Body Mass (g)",
    colour = "Island"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()

mass_compare_plot

Comparing body mass across species, standard deviation shows the variation in individual body mass within each group. Larger error bars indicate larger variation in individual body size within a group, whereas smaller error bars indicate body masses to be more similar within a group. However, standard error provides a better estimate of the precision of the mean, especially when sample sizes differ.

Ecological Interpretation

Mean body mass differed considerably among penguin species. Gentoo penguins have substantially greater body mass when compares to Adelie and Chinstrap penguins. Adelie penguins showed consistant body mass across Biscoe, Dream, and Torgersen Islands, indicating little geographic variation in body size. These patterns suggest that differences in species specific morphology have a greater influence on body size, then geographic differences within this dataset.

Workshop 2: Estuary Fish Survey

Introduction

This workshop focused on cleaning, integrating, and visualising fish survey data collected across four sampling sites in the Ross River Estuary, Townsville, Queensland. Fish catch records from the four sites were combined with site metadata, taxonomic information, and environmental sensor measurements to gain insight into the spatial variation and distribution of fish abundance. Additional consideration was given to salinity gradients and differences in fish abundance between estuarine zones.

Median salinity increased progressively from upstream (4.99 ppt) to marine (34.02 ppt) zones. Standard deviations were relatively low across all four zones (0.10–0.39 ppt), indicating limited variation in salinity measurements within each zone. Though low, the middle-estuary zone exhibited the highest standard deviation value (SD = 0.39 ppt), suggesting slightly higher variability in salinity conditions.

Salinity Gradient Across Estuarine Zones

As expected, salinity differed significantly across the four estuarine zones, reflecting the transition from upstream estuarine conditions to marine conditions. This establishes the environmental salinity gradient that may influence fish abundance and distribution patterns across the Ross River estuary. The error bars indicate the standard deviation of salinity measurements within each zone, highlighting the relative consistency of salinity conditions.

To evaluate the patterns of fish abundance across the estuarine zones, we calculated the mean catch and standard error per species within each estuarine zone. Fish Abundance Patterns Across the Estuarine Gradient

Ecological Interpretation of Fish Abundance

Fish abundance varied across the estuarine gradient, with some species showing clear differences in abundance among zones. Most notably, Lates calcarifer (Barramundi) exhibited substantially greater abundance in the upstream zone, where its mean abundance was nearly twice that of the other species observed. In contrast, Platycephalus spp. (Flathead) were most abundant in the marine zone, again exhibiting approximately twice the abundance of most other species within that zone. Lutjanus argentimaculatus (Mangrove Jack) and Acanthopagrus spp. (Bream) were more evenly distributed across the estuarine gradient, with relatively minor variation in abundance between zones. These patterns suggest that environmental factors, sych as salinity and habitat structure, may influence fish distribution within the Ross River Estuary. Overall, the observed differences highlight the importance of considering spatial variation in environmental conditions when assessing estuarine fish communities.
